01 · Model Predictive Control

MPC is a feedback controller that, at every time step, solves a small optimisation problem: "given where I am now, what is the best plan for the next N steps?" It applies only the first move of that plan, then measures again and re-plans.

Your practical · P1

Gantry crane: you designed Q and R (Bryson's rule), saw LQR be conservative (7.3 s), then built the batch QP and got 5.4 s with MPC running on the swing limits.

ELI5

MPC explained like you're five

🚗 Driving with headlights (receding horizon)

At night you can only see a few metres ahead. You plan for what you can see, drive a little, then look again and re-plan. You never follow an old plan blindly, because the road may have changed.

In your P1: the crane re-solved its QP every 0.1 s and only ever used uMPCout(1), the first move of each plan.

⚖️ Q vs R: two things you hate

Q says how much you hate being away from the target. R says how much you hate pushing hard. Big Q/R: rush there, push hard. Small Q/R: stroll there gently.

In your P1: Q = I with LQR flung the load to 46°. Putting 500 on θ and 50 on ω told the controller "I really hate swinging", and the swing dropped to 8.7°.

🏁 QF: a promise about after the window

Your headlights end somewhere. QF is a price on where you are when they end, so the plan doesn't park you at the edge of a cliff just past what you can see. A good QF is what makes MPC stable.

In your P1: with no terminal term and only a 1 s window, the cart rushed, saw the target too late and the QP went infeasible.

🚧 Constraints: rules, not wishes

LQR can only be persuaded to stay safe by changing weights. MPC treats the limits as hard rules (≤ 3 m/s, ≤ 10° swing), so it can drive right up to them and no further.

In your P1: LQR played safe and took 7.3 s; MPC rode exactly on the 10° and 50°/s limits and took 5.4 s.

1.1 The setup

We have a model \(x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k)\) with \(f(0,0)=0\) (the origin is where we want to be), and possibly constraints \(x_k\in\mathbb X\), \(u_k\in\mathbb U\). The notation \(x_{k+\ell|k}\) means "the state at time \(k+\ell\) predicted using information at time \(k\)". The cost is:

\[J(x_k,\vec u)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\Big(x_{k+\ell|k}^\top Q\,x_{k+\ell|k}+u_{k+\ell|k}^\top R\,u_{k+\ell|k}\Big)+x_{k+N|k}^\top Q_F\,x_{k+N|k}\]

where \(\vec u=[u_{k|k},\dots,u_{k+N-1|k}]\) is the sequence of N future inputs we choose. In general form it is \(\sum L(x,u)+F(x_{k+N|k})\). The design parameters are L, F and N.

1.2 What Q, R and QF do

Q ≥ 0 · state weight

Penalises being away from the target. Bigger Q ⇒ the controller works harder to drive x to 0 quickly. Diagonal entries say which states matter: \(Q=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\) cares only about position, not velocity.

R > 0 · input weight

Penalises control effort (fuel, energy, actuator wear). Bigger R ⇒ smaller, gentler inputs and slower response. R must be positive definite so the problem has a unique minimiser.

QF ≥ 0 · terminal weight

Penalises where the plan ends. It stands in for all the cost after the horizon, so a short-sighted plan doesn't leave the state somewhere bad. Choosing it well (e.g. from a Lyapunov or Riccati equation) gives closed-loop stability.

Only the ratio matters for the shape of the response. With \(R=\rho I\): small ρ (Q large relative to R) ⇒ fast convergence with large inputs; large ρ ⇒ slow convergence with small inputs. The notes show this with a double integrator (ρ = 0.3 vs ρ = 10): the input and output energies \(J_{in}\) and \(J_{out}\) trade off along a curve.

Try it · scalar MPC tuner

Plant \(x_{k+1}=1.2\,x_k+u_k\) (unstable on its own), Q = 1, QF = 1, R = ρ, starting from x0 = 1. Each step, MPC solves the N-step problem and applies the first input.

Blue: state xk. Orange: input uk. Try N = 1 with ρ above 5: the closed loop goes unstable, because a 1-step plan can't see that "saving effort now" lets the state grow. Increase N and it recovers. That is the point of the horizon and of QF.

1.3 Receding horizon optimisation

  1. Measure the current state \(x_k\).
  2. Solve \(\vec u^*_k=\arg\min_{\vec u\in\mathbb U^N}J(x_k,\vec u)\): a full N-step plan.
  3. Apply only the first element: \(u_k=u^*_{k|k}=[I_m\ 0\ \cdots\ 0]\,\vec u^*_k\).
  4. Move to k+1, measure \(x_{k+1}\), and solve again over a window shifted one step forward.

Because it re-solves from the measured state each time, MPC is a feedback law \(u_k=\kappa(x_k)\), even though each optimisation produces an open-loop sequence. In general \(u^*_{k+1|k}\neq u^*_{k+1|k+1}\): the plan made at k for time k+1 is not what is applied at k+1.

Simple example

\(x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k\), N = 2, Q = R = QF = 1, start at \(x_0=10\). Solving (via the Riccati recursion in tab 02) gives the plan rule \(u_{k|k}=-0.6x_k\), \(u_{k+1|k}=-0.5x_{k+1|k}\).

  • At k = 0: plan \(u_{0|0}=-6\Rightarrow x_{1|0}=4\), then \(u_{1|0}=-2\Rightarrow x_{2|0}=2\). Apply only −6.
  • Suppose wind pushes the car: the measured \(x_1=5\), not 4.
  • At k = 1: re-plan from 5: \(u_{1|1}=-0.6\cdot5=-3\). The old plan (−2) would have been wrong for the real state.
  • Even with no disturbance (\(x_1=4\)), \(u_{1|1}=-2.4\neq u_{1|0}=-2\), because the window now looks one step further ahead.

Everyday analogy: driving while looking 100 m ahead. You plan your steering for the whole 100 m, turn the wheel a little, then look again and re-plan with fresh information.

Comments from the notes: MPC works for nonlinear, constrained, even stochastic or simulation-based models. Longer N is usually better (considers long-term impact) but costs more computation; moderate N is often close to infinite-horizon performance. Nonlinear models need numerical optimisation. Integral action can be added by penalising increments \(u_{k+\ell+1|k}-u_{k+\ell|k}\). With both state and input constraints the problem can become infeasible.

1.4 LTI system, quadratic cost: the explicit solution

With \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\) and no constraints, stack the predictions: \(x_{k+\ell|k}=A^\ell x_k+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}A^{\ell-j-1}Bu_{k+j|k}\), so

\[\vec x_k=\begin{bmatrix}x_{k+1|k}\\ \vdots\\ x_{k+N|k}\end{bmatrix}=\Phi\vec u+\Lambda x_k,\quad \Phi=\begin{bmatrix}B&0&\cdots&0\\ AB&B&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\ A^{N-1}B&\cdots&AB&B\end{bmatrix},\quad \Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}A\\A^2\\ \vdots\\A^N\end{bmatrix}\]

With \(\bar Q=\mathrm{diag}(Q,\dots,Q,Q_F)\) and \(R=\rho I\):

\[J=x_k^\top(\Lambda^\top\bar Q\Lambda+Q)x_k+\vec u^\top(\Phi^\top\bar Q\Phi+\rho I)\vec u+2\vec u^\top\Phi^\top\bar Q\Lambda x_k\]

Set \(\partial J/\partial\vec u=0\) (using \(\partial(u^\top Xu)/\partial u=2Xu\), \(\partial(a^\top u)/\partial u=a\)):

\[\vec u^*_k=-(\Phi^\top\bar Q\Phi+\rho I)^{-1}\Phi^\top\bar Q\Lambda\,x_k,\qquad u_k=-Kx_k,\quad K=[I\ 0\cdots0](\Phi^\top\bar Q\Phi+\rho I)^{-1}\Phi^\top\bar Q\Lambda\]

A constant-gain state feedback, identical to finite-horizon LQR. The Hessian is positive definite because ρ > 0, so this is the unique minimum.

Simple example · batch form

Scalar \(a=2,\ b=1\), N = 2, Q = QF = 1, ρ = 1. Then \(\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\end{bmatrix}\), \(\bar Q=I\).

\(H=\Phi^\top\Phi+I=\begin{bmatrix}6&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\Phi^\top\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}10\\4\end{bmatrix}\), \(H^{-1}=\tfrac18\begin{bmatrix}2&-2\\-2&6\end{bmatrix}\), so \(\vec u^*=-\begin{bmatrix}1.5\\0.5\end{bmatrix}x_k\).

MPC applies \(u_k=-1.5x_k\), and the closed loop is \(x_{k+1}=(2-1.5)x_k=0.5x_k\): stable.

With constraints (on position gaps, velocity, acceleration, input) the problem becomes a quadratic programme: \(\min_{\vec u}\ \vec u^\top H\vec u+\vec u^\top f\) subject to \(A_{in}\vec u\le B_{in}\), \(A_{eq}\vec u=B_{eq}\). Constraints on predicted states are converted to constraints on \(\vec u\) by substituting \(\vec x=\Phi\vec u+\Lambda x_k\) (the notes build them with Kronecker products, e.g. \(\mathbf 1_N\otimes[1\ 0\ 0]\) picks out the position).

Discretisation: double integrator \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(B=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta t^2/2\\ \Delta t\end{bmatrix}\). In general \(\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\0&I\end{bmatrix}=\exp\!\Big(\begin{bmatrix}A_c&B_c\\0&0\end{bmatrix}T_s\Big)\).

1.5 Stability (optional in notes, good for theory marks)

Assumption 1: \(F(0)=0,\ F\ge0,\ L(0,0)=0,\ L(x,u)\ge\alpha(\|x\|)\) with α increasing, α(0) = 0.
Assumption 2: there is a local controller \(\kappa_f\) with \(F(f(x,\kappa_f(x)))\le F(x)-L(x,\kappa_f(x))\). The terminal cost acts like a Lyapunov function.

Proof idea: let \(V_k=J(x_k,\vec u^*_k)\). At k+1, a feasible (not necessarily optimal) plan is "the rest of the old plan, plus \(\kappa_f\) at the end". Its cost is at most \(V_k-L(x_k,u^*_{k|k})\). Optimal is no worse, so \(0\le V_{k+1}\le V_k-\alpha(\|x_k\|)\). V is decreasing and bounded below ⇒ converges ⇒ \(\alpha(\|x_k\|)\to0\) ⇒ \(x_k\to0\).

Finite input set (e.g. power converters, quantisers): pick R = 0, \(\kappa_f=0\), and \(Q_F\) solving the Lyapunov equation \(A^\top Q_FA+Q=Q_F\) (requires A stable). For scalar a: \(Q_F=q/(1-a^2)\).

1.6 Case study: vehicle platooning (optional)

Each car: \(\dot p=v,\ \dot v=a,\ \dot a=-a/\tau+u/\tau\) (τ ≈ 1.5 actuation lag). Track the gap error \(e_{i,k}=x_{i-1,k}-x_{i,k}\), which has the same dynamics \(e_{k+1}=Ae_k+B\bar u_k\), with \(u_i=u_{i-1}-\bar u_i\). Infinite-horizon LQR (DARE) ignores constraints: in the notes vehicle 3 crashes into vehicle 2 and speeds reach 288 km/h. MPC adds \(p_{min}=3\) m, \(0\le v\le25\) m/s, \(-6\le a\le3\) m/s² and solves a QP, so the cars keep their gaps and respect the speed limit.

02 · Deterministic Dynamic Programming

DP solves a multi-stage decision problem by solving it backwards from the end. The best decision at every state and every time comes out of one backward sweep.

Your practical · P2

Tom's journey A→H by backward DP (1063, A-B-F-H; the greedy route costs 1305) and the 4-city TSP, nearest neighbour and rollout.

ELI5

Deterministic DP explained like you're five

🔙 Plan the trip backwards

Stand at the destination and ask every city one question: "how much does it cost to get from here to the end, at best?" Write that on a price tag. Cities next to the end are easy; each earlier city just adds its road cost to the next city's tag and keeps the cheapest.

In your P2: tags E 807, F 450, G 603, then B 730, C 854, D 1056, then A = 1063 via B.

🧩 Principle of optimality

If the best route from A to H goes through F, then the part from F to H must itself be the best route from F. Otherwise you could swap in a better ending and beat the "best" route. That's why the tags can be reused.

In your P2: all three best paths go through F, the cheapest "hub" into H.

🍬 The greedy trap

Grabbing the cheapest road right now is like eating all your sweets first: nice now, painful later. Always compare road cost + price tag of where it leads.

In your P2: A→D (230) looked cheapest but led to a 1305 trip, 5th of 9.

💥 Curse of dimensionality

DP is exact, but the number of price tags can explode. A salesperson visiting 20 cities has about 6×1016 possible tours; DP needs about 4×108 steps. At 50 cities even DP gives up, so we approximate (tab 05).

In your P2: the TSP DP state was (where am I, which cities are left).

2.1 Principle of optimality

Bellman's principle

"An optimal trajectory has the property that at an intermediate point, no matter how it was reached, the rest of the trajectory must coincide with an optimal trajectory as computed from this intermediate point as the starting point."

In plain words: if the best route Brisbane → Sydney passes through Coffs Harbour, then the Coffs Harbour → Sydney part must itself be the best route from Coffs Harbour. So solve the short "tail" problems first and build longer ones from them.

2.3 The DP algorithm

System \(x_{k+1}=f_k(x_k,u_k)\), cost \(J(x_0,\vec u)=g_N(x_N)+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}g_k(x_k,u_k)\). The cost-to-go \(J_k(x_k)\) is the optimal cost of the tail problem from \(x_k\) at time k.

\[J_N(x_N)=g_N(x_N),\qquad J_k(x_k)=\min_{u_k\in\mathbb U}\Big\{g_k(x_k,u_k)+J_{k+1}\big(f_k(x_k,u_k)\big)\Big\},\quad k=N-1,\dots,0\]

The optimal cost is \(J^*(x_0)=J_0(x_0)\). To recover the actions, go forward: \(u_0^*=\arg\min\{g_0(x_0,u)+J_1(f_0(x_0,u))\}\), \(x_1^*=f_0(x_0,u_0^*)\), and so on.

Catch: \(J_k\) must be computed for every state, because we don't know in advance which states we'll visit. Continuous states must be discretised, and large state spaces or long horizons get expensive (the "curse of dimensionality"). This motivates tabs 05 and 06.

2.4 LQR solved by DP

For \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\), \(g_k=x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru\), \(g_N=x^\top Q_Fx\), the cost-to-go stays quadratic: \(J_k(x)=x^\top S_kx\).

\[S_N=Q_F,\qquad S_k=A^\top S_{k+1}A+Q-A^\top S_{k+1}B(R+B^\top S_{k+1}B)^{-1}B^\top S_{k+1}A\] \[u_k^*=L_kx_k,\qquad L_k=-(R+B^\top S_{k+1}B)^{-1}B^\top S_{k+1}A,\qquad J^*(x_0)=x_0^\top S_0x_0\]

Why (one step): \(J_{N-1}(x)=\min_u\{x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru+(Ax+Bu)^\top S_N(Ax+Bu)\}\). The bracket is quadratic in u. Setting its derivative to zero gives \(u=L_{N-1}x\), and substituting back gives \(x^\top S_{N-1}x\). Induction does the rest.

Scalar version (a, b, q, r): \(S_k=q+\dfrac{a^2rS_{k+1}}{r+b^2S_{k+1}}\), \(L_k=-\dfrac{abS_{k+1}}{r+b^2S_{k+1}}\).

Simple example

\(a=b=q=r=1\), \(Q_F=1\), N = 2. \(S_2=1\). \(S_1=1+\frac{1\cdot1}{1+1}=1.5\). \(S_0=1+\frac{1.5}{2.5}=1.6\).

Gains: \(L_1=-\frac{1}{2}=-0.5\), \(L_0=-\frac{1.5}{2.5}=-0.6\). Optimal cost \(J^*=1.6x_0^2\). These are the numbers used in the MPC example in tab 01: MPC with N = 2 applies \(L_0=-0.6\) at every step.

Be ready to (i) run DP on a small graph or table and state the policy, (ii) do one or two Riccati steps by hand for a scalar system, and (iii) explain in words why DP works backwards (principle of optimality).

03 · Stochastic Dynamic Programming

Now the next state depends on a random disturbance: \(x_{k+1}=f_k(x_k,u_k,w_k)\). We can't control exactly where we go, so we minimise the expected cost and choose a policy (a rule) instead of a fixed list of actions.

Your practical · P3

Random leg costs (certainty equivalence gives 1063 again), a random road closure (go West, 1013) and the parking stopping problem (threshold rules).

ELI5

Stochastic DP explained like you're five

□ = you choose · ○ = chance chooses. Average at each ○, take the best at each □. (Your P3 Part B.)

🎲 Choose before the dice, react after

Some choices are made before luck shows its hand, so you judge them by their average. Later choices are made after you see what happened, so you just pick the best.

In your P3: East or West is chosen on averages (1210.4 vs 1013), but at D or E Tom simply picks the cheapest next city.

📜 A policy, not a plan

A plan says "go A, D, F, H". A policy says "if you end up at D go to F; if at E go to G". With randomness you need the rule for every outcome, because you don't know where you'll be.

In your P3: the output ['West','G','F','F','G',…] is exactly that list of rules.

📊 "Expected" means "on average over many trips"

1063 is what Tom pays on average; any single trip on A-B-F-H can cost from 430 to 2030. DP with E{·} doesn't care about that spread, only the average.

In your P3: spreading B→F's costs but keeping the mean changed nothing. Moving the mean changed the path.

🌫️ Noise is a tax you can't dodge

If the state is pushed by noise w with variance σ², a squared cost picks up an extra σ² you can't control: E{(a+w)²} = a² + σ². The best control is the same as without noise, but the cost goes up by σ² per stage.

2024 Q2: exactly this, which is why J0 = β(x0² + 3σ²).

🅿️ Stopping problems: take it or keep looking

Parking: take this spot, or gamble that a better one is ahead? Compare "stop now" with "the average cost of carrying on". The answer is usually a threshold: keep looking until about here, then take the first one you see.

In your P3: with c = 1 it was "take the first free spot"; with cheap search the threshold moved later (k* = 1, 5, 7, 8).

3.1 Policies, not sequences

A policy is \(\pi=\{\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{N-1}\}\) with \(u_k=\mu_k(x_k)\). It uses the state we will actually observe later, so it reacts to the noise that happened. For stochastic systems policies typically beat fixed sequences. For deterministic systems they are equivalent. We minimise

\[J_\pi(x_0)=\mathbb E\Big\{g_N(x_N)+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}g_k\big(x_k,\mu_k(x_k),w_k\big)\Big\}\]

Assumption: \(w_k\)'s distribution may depend on \(x_k,u_k\) but not on earlier w's.

3.2 The stochastic DP algorithm

\[J_N(x)=g_N(x),\qquad J_k(x)=\min_{u\in\mathbb U}\ \mathbb E_{w_k}\Big\{g_k(x,u,w_k)+J_{k+1}\big(f_k(x,u,w_k)\big)\Big\}\]

The only change from tab 02 is the expectation. If \(u_k^*=\mu_k^*(x_k)\) minimises the right side for every x and k, then \(\pi^*=\{\mu_0^*,\dots\}\) is optimal. You can compute the policy offline and store it, or store \(J_k\) and minimise online.

The expectation trick (most of exam Q2)

If \(\mathbb E\{w\}=0\), \(\mathbb E\{w^2\}=\sigma^2\), and w is uncorrelated with x and u, then for any expression a built from x and u:

\[\mathbb E\{(a+w)^2\}=a^2+2a\,\mathbb E\{w\}+\mathbb E\{w^2\}=a^2+\sigma^2\]

The noise adds a constant σ² that does not depend on u, so it doesn't change the best u. It only raises the cost. Vector version: \(\mathbb E\{w^\top Sw\}=\mathrm{tr}(S\Sigma)\).

Worked example (same structure as 2024 Q2)

\(x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k+w_k\), \(g_k=\beta x_k^2\), \(g_N=\beta x_N^2\), N = 3.

  • \(J_3(x)=\beta x^2\).
  • \(J_2(x)=\min_u\mathbb E\{\beta x^2+\beta(x+u+w)^2\}=\beta x^2+\beta\min_u(x+u)^2+\beta\sigma^2\). Minimised at \(u=-x\), so \(J_2(x)=\beta x^2+\beta\sigma^2\).
  • \(J_1(x)=\beta x^2+\min_u\mathbb E\{\beta(x+u+w)^2+\beta\sigma^2\}=\beta x^2+2\beta\sigma^2\), with \(u=-x\).
  • \(J_0(x)=\beta x^2+3\beta\sigma^2\), with \(u=-x\).

Pattern: \(J_k(x)=\beta x^2+(N-k)\beta\sigma^2\). Each remaining step adds one unit of unavoidable noise cost \(\beta\sigma^2\).

3.3 LQR with noise and with packet dropouts

Additive noise \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k+w_k\): the gains \(L_k\) are exactly the deterministic ones (certainty equivalence). The cost increases by \(\sum_k\mathbb E\{w_k^\top S_{k+1}w_k\}\).

Intermittent communication: \(B_k=B\) with probability Γ, and \(B_k=0\) with probability 1−Γ (wireless dropouts). Averaging over \(B_k\) gives \(\mathbb E\{B_k\}=\Gamma B\) and \(\mathbb E\{B_k^\top SB_k\}=\Gamma B^\top SB\), so

\[L_k=-(R+\Gamma B^\top S_{k+1}B)^{-1}\Gamma B^\top S_{k+1}A,\quad S_k=A^\top S_{k+1}A+Q-A^\top S_{k+1}\Gamma B(R+\Gamma B^\top S_{k+1}B)^{-1}\Gamma B^\top S_{k+1}A\]

Γ = 1 recovers ordinary LQR.

3.4 Markov Decision Processes

When states and actions are finite, describe the randomness with transition probabilities instead of an equation:

\[p_{ij}(u)=\Pr(x_{k+1}=j\mid x_k=i,\ u_k=u),\qquad\sum_j p_{ij}(u)=1\]

The DP recursion becomes a weighted sum: \(J_k(i)=\min_u\big\{g(i,u)+\sum_jp_{ij}(u)J_{k+1}(j)\big\}\). (Any MDP can be written as \(x_{k+1}=w_k\), and any \(f(x,u,w)\) model gives \(p_{ij}\).)

4.1 Discounted cost

\[J_\pi(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E\Big\{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\alpha^kg\big(x_k,\mu_k(x_k),w_k\big)\Big\},\qquad \alpha\in[0,1)\]
  • α = 0: myopic, only today's cost counts. α near 1: far-sighted, future costs matter almost as much (and algorithms converge more slowly).
  • Discounting keeps the infinite sum finite: if \(|g|\le M\) then \(|J|\le M/(1-\alpha)\). Useful identity: \(\sum_{k\ge0}\alpha^k=\frac1{1-\alpha}\). Example: cost 1 every step with α = 0.9 ⇒ total 10.
  • Key recursive property: \(J_\pi(x_0)=\mathbb E\{g(x_0,\mu(x_0),w_0)\}+\alpha J_\pi(x_1)\). Today's cost plus the discounted cost from tomorrow.
  • For shortest-path problems that end in finite time, α = 1 is allowed.

4.2 Bellman's equation

\[J^*(x)=\min_{u}\ \mathbb E_w\Big\{g(x,u,w)+\alpha J^*\big(f(x,u,w)\big)\Big\}\qquad\text{finite: }J^*(i)=\min_u\Big\{g(i,u)+\alpha\sum_jp_{ij}(u)J^*(j)\Big\}\]

\(J^*\) is the fixed point of the Bellman operator \(TJ(x)=\min_u\mathbb E\{g+\alpha J(f)\}\). The minimising u at each x is the optimal stationary policy \(\mu^*\). For a fixed policy μ: \(J_\mu=T_\mu J_\mu\), i.e. \(J_\mu(i)=g(i,\mu(i))+\alpha\sum_jp_{ij}(\mu(i))J_\mu(j)\).

4.3 Value Iteration (VI)

Start from any bounded \(V_0\) (usually 0) and apply Bellman's equation repeatedly:

\[V_{k+1}(i)=\min_u\Big\{g(i,u)+\alpha\sum_jp_{ij}(u)V_k(j)\Big\}\qquad\text{matrix form: }V_{k+1}=\min\{G(0)+\alpha P(0)V_k,\ G(1)+\alpha P(1)V_k\}\]

(min taken row by row). \(V_k\) is the optimal k-stage cost. It is DP run forwards with the time index reversed. \(V_k\to J^*\) as k → ∞ (T is a contraction: the error shrinks like \(\alpha^K M/(1-\alpha)\)). The greedy policy often settles before the values do, but that is not guaranteed.

Try it · VI on the Rover

4.4 Policy Iteration (PI)

  1. Policy evaluation (critic): for the current policy μ, solve the linear equations \(J_\mu=g_\mu+\alpha P_\mu J_\mu\), i.e. \(J_\mu=(I-\alpha P_\mu)^{-1}g_\mu\). Or iterate \(J_{\mu,k+1}=g_\mu+\alpha P_\mu J_{\mu,k}\) until it settles.
  2. Policy improvement (actor): \(\mu^{new}(i)=\arg\min_u\{g(i,u)+\alpha\sum_jp_{ij}(u)J_\mu(j)\}\).
  3. If the policy didn't change, stop: it is optimal. Otherwise go to 1.

There are finitely many policies and each iteration improves, so PI stops in finitely many steps. For the Rover with α = 0.96 it converges within 2 improvements (notes, Table 1): μ = (0,0,0) → (0,1,0) → (0,1,1).

5.1 Approximation in value space

\[\tilde u_k\in\arg\min_{u}\Big\{g_k(x_k,u)+\tilde J_{k+1}\big(f_k(x_k,u)\big)\Big\}\]

No backward pass: the controls are computed going forward, because \(\tilde J\) is assumed available. If \(\tilde J=J\) exactly, you get the optimal control. The better \(\tilde J\), the better the policy. \(\tilde J\) can come from intuition (a heuristic), from rollout, or from a trained parametric model.

5.2 Rollout

Pick a simple base policy (a heuristic). To evaluate each candidate action, apply it, then "roll out" the base policy to the end and add up the cost. That total is \(\tilde J\). Choose the action with the lowest total. Then move one step and repeat.

Cost improvement property: the rollout policy is never worse than the base policy (typically strictly better). The name comes from Tesauro's backgammon program, which "rolled out" many games from a position.

5.3 Stochastic problems and certainty-equivalent control

With noise there are two things to approximate: the cost-to-go and the expectation \(\mathbb E_w\{\cdot\}\). Certainty-equivalent control (CEC) replaces the random \(w_k\) by one typical value \(\bar w_k\) (usually \(\mathbb E\{w_k\}\)), giving a deterministic problem:

\[\min_u\Big\{g_k(x_k,u,\bar w_k)+\tilde J_{k+1}\big(f_k(x_k,u,\bar w_k)\big)\Big\}\]

For linear systems with quadratic cost and Gaussian noise (LQG), CEC is exactly optimal, as you saw in tab 03 where noise didn't change the LQR gain.

Simple example

\(x_1=x+u+w\) with \(\mathbb E w=1\), cost \(u^2+x_1^2\). CEC: set w = 1 and minimise \(u^2+(x+u+1)^2\Rightarrow u=-(x+1)/2\). The true expected cost is \(u^2+(x+u+1)^2+\mathrm{Var}(w)\), which has the same minimiser, so CEC is exact here because the cost is quadratic.

5.4 Multistep lookahead and truncated rollout

Look ℓ steps ahead instead of one: optimise \(u_k,\mu_{k+1},\dots,\mu_{k+\ell-1}\) for \(\mathbb E\{\sum_{j=k}^{k+\ell-1}g_j+\tilde J_{k+\ell}(x_{k+\ell})\}\), apply only the first control, and repeat (exactly the MPC idea). Large ℓ compensates for a poor \(\tilde J\) but costs more computation. Truncated rollout: run the base policy for ℓ−1 steps, then add a terminal approximation \(\tilde J_{k+\ell}\). If ℓ is large, \(\tilde J=0\) often works. For infinite horizon: \(\tilde\mu(x)\in\arg\min_u\mathbb E\{g(x,u,w)+\alpha\tilde J(f(x,u,w))\}\).

5.5 Parametric approximation

Choose a family \(\tilde J(x,r)\) with tunable parameters r (polynomials, Gaussian bases, neural networks). Simplest and most common: features \(\phi(x)=[\phi_1(x),\dots,\phi_m(x)]^\top\) (handcrafted, e.g. aggregated states) and a linear architecture

\[\tilde J(x,r)=r^\top\phi(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^m r_\ell\,\phi_\ell(x)\qquad(r=\text{weights})\]

Training: collect samples \((x^s,\beta^s)\) where \(\beta^s\) is a (noisy) cost estimate at \(x^s\), and minimise the squared error

\[\Phi(r)=\tfrac12\sum_{s=1}^q\big(\tilde J(x^s,r)-\beta^s\big)^2\]

For the linear architecture this is convex, and setting the gradient to zero gives least squares:

\[\hat r=\Big(\sum_s\phi(x^s)\phi(x^s)^\top\Big)^{-1}\sum_s\beta^s\phi(x^s)\]

Or gradient descent: \(r^{\ell+1}=r^\ell-\gamma^\ell\sum_s\phi(x^s)\big(r^{\ell\top}\phi(x^s)-\beta^s\big)\), with a decreasing step size \(\gamma^\ell\). Stochastic GD uses one (or a few) random samples per step: cheaper and often faster. For finite horizons you fit a separate \(r_k\) for each stage, working backwards. Infinite horizon needs only one r.

Simple example

Features \(\phi(x)=[1,\ x]^\top\), samples (x, β) = (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 7).

\(\sum\phi\phi^\top=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\), \(\sum\beta\phi=\begin{bmatrix}11\\17\end{bmatrix}\), inverse \(=\frac16\begin{bmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix}\) ⇒ \(\hat r=\frac16\begin{bmatrix}4\\18\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.667\\3\end{bmatrix}\). So \(\tilde J(x)=0.667+3x\).

One GD step from r = 0 with γ = 0.1: gradient \(=-[11,17]^\top\) ⇒ \(r^1=[1.1,\ 1.7]^\top\).

The traditional alternative is system identification (fit a model from data, then use MPC/DP). RL skips the explicit model. It is also called neuro-dynamic programming.

6.1 Temporal-difference (TD) prediction

Goal: estimate \(J_\mu(x)\) for a fixed policy μ from data. Bellman says \(J_\mu(x)=\mathbb E\{g+\alpha J_\mu(x')\}\), so each observed \(g+\alpha J(x')\) is an unbiased sample of \(J_\mu(x)\). Nudge the estimate toward it:

\[J(x_k)\leftarrow J(x_k)+\delta_k\Big[\underbrace{g(x_k,\mu(x_k),w_k)+\alpha J(x_{k+1})}_{\text{TD target}}-J(x_k)\Big]\]

The bracket is the TD error: how surprised we are. δ is the learning rate. It is a Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation scheme.

Simple example

J = 0 everywhere, α = 0.9, δ = 0.5. Observed: A (cost 2) → B (cost 0) → A (cost 2) → B.
1) \(J(A)=0+0.5(2+0.9\cdot0-0)=1\). 2) \(J(B)=0+0.5(0+0.9\cdot1-0)=0.45\). 3) \(J(A)=1+0.5(2+0.9\cdot0.45-1)=1.7025\).

6.2 Action-value functions (Q-factors)

\[Q_\mu(x,u)=\mathbb E\{g(x,u,w)+\alpha J_\mu(f(x,u,w))\}\]

"Cost of doing u now, then following μ." The optimal Q-factor gives both the value and the policy without a model:

\[J^*(x)=\min_uQ^*(x,u),\qquad\mu^*(x)=\arg\min_uQ^*(x,u),\qquad Q^*(x,u)=\mathbb E\Big\{g(x,u,w)+\alpha\min_{u'}Q^*(f(x,u,w),u')\Big\}\]

The last equation is the Q-Bellman equation, the basis of Q-learning.

6.3 Sarsa (on-policy)

\[Q(x,u)\leftarrow Q(x,u)+\delta\Big[g(x,u,w)+\alpha\,Q(x',u')-Q(x,u)\Big]\]

Uses the quintuple (x, u, g, x′, u′): State, Action, Reward, State, Action. Here u′ is the action actually taken next. Algorithm 1: measure x, pick u (ε-greedy); loop: apply u, observe g and x′, pick u′ (ε-greedy), update Q(x,u), set x←x′, u←u′. Only the visited entry changes. Terminal states start (and stay) at Q = 0.

6.4 Exploration: ε-greedy

With probability 1−ε pick the greedy action \(\arg\min_uQ(x,u)\) (exploit). With probability ε pick a random action (explore). Since the random pick can also land on the greedy action, P(greedy) = 1 − ε + ε/|U|. ε usually decreases over time, e.g. \(\varepsilon_k=1/k\) or \(\max(1/k,0.01)\). Without exploration some actions are never tried and their Q stays wrong.

6.5 Q-learning (off-policy)

\[Q(x,u)\leftarrow Q(x,u)+\delta\Big[g(x,u,w)+\alpha\min_{u'}Q(x',u')-Q(x,u)\Big]\]

Same as Sarsa except the target uses the best next action, not the one taken. It learns \(Q^*\) (the greedy policy's values) while behaving ε-greedily. That makes it off-policy: the policy being learned differs from the policy being followed. Sarsa learns the values of the policy it actually follows (on-policy), including its exploration, so it tends to be more cautious.

Simple example · one update, two algorithms

Costs. Q(s1,a) = 4, Q(s2,a) = 3, Q(s2,b) = 1. Observe (s1, a, g = 2, s2) and the ε-greedy rule explores and picks a next. δ = 0.5, α = 0.9.
Sarsa: \(4+0.5(2+0.9\cdot Q(s2,a)-4)=4+0.5(0.7)=4.35\).
Q-learning: \(4+0.5(2+0.9\cdot\min\{3,1\}-4)=4+0.5(-1.1)=3.45\).

6.6 Convergence conditions

  • Every (x,u) pair visited infinitely often (enough exploration).
  • Learning rate: \(\delta_k>0,\ \sum_k\delta_k=\infty,\ \sum_k\delta_k^2<\infty\). Example: \(\delta_k=1/k\) works. Constant δ fails the second condition. \(1/k^2\) fails the first (it stops learning too early).
  • For Sarsa to reach \(Q^*\), the policy must become greedy in the limit (ε → 0). With constant ε, Sarsa converges to the Q of the ε-greedy policy.
  • In the Rover experiments: δ = 0.1, \(\varepsilon_k=\max(1/k,0.01)\), 500 episodes of 1000 steps, with Q carried between episodes.

6.7 Bigger problems and caveats

For large spaces use \(\hat Q(x,u;r)\) (e.g. a neural network) and minimise the squared TD error \(\mathbb E\{(g+\alpha\min_u\hat Q(x',u;r)-\hat Q(x,u;r))^2\}\) by gradient descent. This is deep RL (DQN). Convergence of learning does not guarantee closed-loop stability, because learning and plant dynamics are coupled and discounting doesn't force stability. Use expert knowledge (features, known dynamics) and don't learn what you already know.

P6-a · words8 marks

On Cliff Walking, Q-learning learns the path along the cliff edge but SARSA learns a safer, longer path. Explain using the update rules. Which agent earns more reward during training?

Show answer

Q-learning is off-policy: its target uses \(\max_{u'}Q(x',u')\), so it estimates the value of acting greedily, i.e. Q*, and the edge path (−13) is optimal for a greedy agent. SARSA is on-policy: its target uses the action the ε-greedy policy actually takes, so it learns the value of the exploring policy. Next to the cliff, a random "down" costs −100, and SARSA builds that risk into the edge cells' Q-values, so a detour 1–2 rows up (−15 or −17) looks better. During training (while still exploring) SARSA usually earns more reward, because it falls off less often; Q-learning's greedy path is better only once exploration stops.

Show answer

ε = 0: same as Q-learning, the 13-step edge path. Small ε: one row of margin (15 steps). An LQR design meets every limit but settles in 7.3 s and only reaches 1.8 m/s. Explain why LQR is conservative here and how constrained MPC with the receding-horizon principle improves on it.

LQR is a fixed linear law \(u=-K(x-x^*)\); its largest push is at t = 0 when the error is largest (8 m). Question 1 · MPC (25 marks)

Cost \(J(x_k,\vec u)=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(x^\top_{k+l|k}Qx_{k+l|k}+u^\top_{k+l|k}Ru_{k+l|k})+x^\top_{k+N|k}Q_Fx_{k+N|k}\).

(a) Role of Q, QF and R; how they shape control actions and the closed-loop response (10)

Q (≥ 0), stage state weight. Penalises deviation of the predicted states from the origin (the target) at every step of the horizon. Increasing Q makes the optimiser care more about regulation error, so the controller drives the state to zero faster with smaller overshoot or error. The individual entries choose which states matter: e.g. \(Q=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\) penalises position but not velocity.

R (> 0), input weight. Penalises control effort (energy, fuel, actuator use). Increasing R gives smaller, smoother inputs and a slower, more conservative state response. R must be positive definite so the cost is strictly convex in \(\vec u\) and the minimiser is unique (Hessian \(\Phi^\top\bar Q\Phi+R\) > 0).

QF (≥ 0), terminal weight. Penalises the predicted state at the end of the horizon, \(x_{k+N|k}\). It approximates the cost of everything beyond the horizon, so the finite-horizon plan doesn't leave the state somewhere bad. It is the main tool for closed-loop stability: e.g. QF from the Riccati equation (infinite-horizon LQR cost) or from the Lyapunov equation \(A^\top Q_FA+Q=Q_F\) makes the optimal cost decrease along trajectories.

Trade-off. Only the relative size matters. Large Q relative to R (small ρ in R = ρI) ⇒ aggressive control: fast convergence, large inputs, possibly hitting actuator limits. Small Q relative to R ⇒ gentle inputs, slow convergence. The designer picks a point on the trade-off curve between input energy \(J_{in}\) and output energy \(J_{out}\). For an LTI unconstrained system the resulting law is \(u_k=-Kx_k\), and K (hence the closed-loop poles \(A-BK\)) is set by Q, R, QF and N.

(b) Main idea behind receding horizon optimisation (15)

At each sampling time k:

  1. Measure (or estimate) the current state \(x_k\).
  2. Using the model, predict the state over a finite horizon of N steps and solve \(\vec u^*_k=\arg\min_{\vec u\in\mathbb U^N}J(x_k,\vec u)\) subject to the constraints. This gives an optimal open-loop plan \(u^*_{k|k},\dots,u^*_{k+N-1|k}\).
  3. Apply only the first element, \(u_k=u^*_{k|k}\). Discard the rest.
  4. At k+1, measure \(x_{k+1}\), shift the horizon forward by one step (now k+1 … k+N), and repeat.

The horizon "recedes" (moves forward) with time. Because the problem is re-solved from the newly measured state, the controller is a feedback law \(u_k=\kappa(x_k)\). It corrects for disturbances and model mismatch, handles constraints explicitly, and needs only finite-horizon optimisations even for a process that runs forever. Generally \(u^*_{k+1|k}\ne u^*_{k+1|k+1}\).

Example. \(x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k\), N = 2, Q = R = QF = 1, \(x_0=10\). Solving gives \(u_{k|k}=-0.6x_k\), \(u_{k+1|k}=-0.5x_{k+1|k}\).

Model Predictive Control

Q1 · theory8 marks

Why does MPC apply only the first input of the optimal sequence instead of the whole sequence? What would go wrong if it applied the whole sequence open-loop?

Show answer

Applying only the first input and re-optimising at the next step from the measured state turns MPC into a feedback law \(u_k=\kappa(x_k)\). Disturbances, noise and model errors are corrected because each new plan starts from the true state. Applying the whole sequence open-loop would ignore anything that happens after time k: errors would accumulate with no correction, and once the N steps ran out there would be no plan at all. Re-planning also keeps looking N steps ahead (the window slides), which improves performance and keeps constraints satisfied using fresh information.

Q2 · calculation12 marks

Plant \(x_{k+1}=2x_k+u_k\), cost with Q = QF = 1, R = 1. (a) Find the MPC law and closed-loop dynamics for N = 1. (b) Repeat for N = 2. Comment.

Show answer

(a) N = 1: \(J=x^2+u^2+(2x+u)^2\). \(dJ/du=2u+2(2x+u)=0\Rightarrow u=-x\). Closed loop \(x_{k+1}=2x-x=x_k\): the state never decays (marginally stable). Not acceptable.

(b) N = 2: Riccati from \(S_2=1\): \(S_1=1+\frac{4\cdot1\cdot1}{1+1}=3\). \(L_0=-\frac{ab S_1}{r+b^2S_1}=-\frac{2\cdot3}{1+3}=-1.5\). Closed loop \(x_{k+1}=(2-1.5)x_k=0.5x_k\): asymptotically stable. (Batch form gives the same: \(\vec u^*=-[1.5,\ 0.5]^\top x_k\).)

Comment: for an unstable plant a too-short horizon with a weak terminal weight can fail to stabilise. Longer N or a larger/better QF fixes it.

Q16 · CEC6 marks

Explain certainty-equivalent control and when it is optimal.

Show answer

CEC replaces each random disturbance \(w_k\) by a single nominal value \(\bar w_k\) (typically its mean), turning the stochastic DP into a deterministic one that is much cheaper to solve. It removes the expectation operator. It is optimal for linear dynamics with quadratic cost and additive (e.g. Gaussian) noise (LQG), because noise then adds only a constant to the cost-to-go and doesn't change the minimiser. Otherwise it is a suboptimal approximation.

Q21 · theory8 marks

Explain on-policy vs off-policy with Sarsa and Q-learning, and the exploration–exploitation trade-off.

Show answer

Sarsa updates with \(Q(x',u')\) for the action it actually executes (ε-greedy), so it evaluates and improves the same policy it follows: on-policy. Q-learning updates with \(\min_{u'}Q(x',u')\), learning the greedy policy's values while executing a different (ε-greedy) behaviour policy: off-policy. Exploitation picks the action that currently looks best. Exploration tries other actions to discover better ones and to keep every (x,u) visited, which convergence requires. ε-greedy balances the two, usually decreasing ε over time.

1 · MPC (Model Predictive Control)

Model xk+1 = f(xk, uk), x ∈ X ⊆ ℝn, u ∈ U ⊆ ℝm, f(0,0)=0. Prediction xk+ℓ+1|k = f(xk+ℓ|k, uk+ℓ|k), xk|k = xk (measured).

Cost J(xk, u⃗) = Σℓ=0N−1 [xTQx + uTRu]k+ℓ|k + xTk+N|k QF xk+N|k, with Q ≥ 0, QF ≥ 0, R > 0. Design knobs: L, F, N.

Q = weight on state error → how hard we push x to 0 (speed/accuracy). R = weight on input → control effort/energy, actuator wear. QF = terminal weight → stands in for cost after the horizon; key to stability. Large Q/R ⇒ fast convergence, big inputs. Small Q/R ⇒ slow, gentle inputs. Diagonal entries weight individual states (e.g. Q = diag(1,0) penalises position only). R = ρI: ρ trades input energy Jin vs output energy Jout.

Receding horizon: (1) measure xk; (2) solve u⃗*k = argminu⃗∈UN J(xk, u⃗); (3) apply only the first element uk = u*k|k = [Im 0 … 0] u⃗*k; (4) k ← k+1, shift window, repeat. Gives implicit feedback law uk = κ(xk). Usually u*k+1|k ≠ u*k+1|k+1. Why: feedback corrects disturbances/model errors, handles constraints, looks ahead, finite computation. Longer N ⇒ better but more computation; moderate N ≈ infinite-horizon performance.

Batch form (LTI) xk+ℓ|k = Aℓxk + Σj=0ℓ−1 Aℓ−j−1Buk+j|k; x⃗k = Φu⃗ + Λxk, Φ = [B 0 … 0; AB B … 0; …; AN−1B … AB B], Λ = [A; A2; …; AN], Q̄ = diag(Q,…,Q,QF).
J = xkT(ΛTQ̄Λ+Q)xk + u⃗T(ΦTQ̄Φ+ρI)u⃗ + 2u⃗TΦTQ̄Λxk
u⃗* = −(ΦTQ̄Φ + ρI)−1ΦTQ̄Λ xk; uk = −Kxk, K = [I 0…0](ΦTQ̄Φ+ρI)−1ΦTQ̄Λ = finite-horizon LQR. ∂(uTXu)/∂u = 2Xu, ∂(aTu)/∂u = a.

Constraints → QP: min u⃗THu⃗ + u⃗Tf s.t. Ainu⃗ ≤ Bin (may be infeasible). Integral action: penalise Δu = uk+ℓ+1 − uk+ℓ. Double integrator: A = [1 Δt; 0 1], B = [Δt²/2; Δt]. ZOH: [A B; 0 I] = exp([Ac Bc; 0 0]Ts).

Stability (opt.) A1: F(0)=0, F≥0, L(0,0)=0, L(x,u) ≥ α(‖x‖). A2: ∃κf: F(f(x,κf(x))) ≤ F(x) − L(x,κf(x)). Shifted sequence [u*k+1|k,…,κf(x*k+N|k)] ⇒ 0 ≤ Vk+1 ≤ Vk − α(‖xk‖) ⇒ xk → 0. Finite input set: R=0, κf=0, QF from Lyapunov ATQFA + Q = QF (A stable; scalar QF = q/(1−a²)).

Platoon ei,k = xi−1,k − xi,k, ek+1 = Aek + Būk + wk, ui = ui−1 − ūi. DARE: S = ATSA − ATSB(R+BTSB)−1BTSA + Q, K = (R+BTSB)−1BTSA. LQR ignores constraints (crash, 288 km/h); MPC adds pmin, vmax, a, u limits.

Principle of optimality: the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the tail subproblem starting from that point, no matter how it was reached.

J(x0,u⃗) = gN(xN) + Σk=0N−1 gk(xk,uk). DP: JN(x) = gN(x); Jk(x) = minu∈U{gk(x,u) + Jk+1(fk(x,u))}, k = N−1…0 (backward, all states). J* = J0(x0). Forward pass: u*k = argmin{…}, x*k+1 = f(x*k,u*k). Curse of dimensionality: needs every state ⇒ discretise.

4 · Infinite horizon: VI & PI

Jπ(x) = lim E{Σk αkg(xk,μk(xk),wk)}, α ∈ [0,1). α=0 myopic; α→1 far-sighted (slower convergence). Stationary policies. Jπ(x0) = E{g(x0,…)} + αJπ(x1). Σk≥0αk = 1/(1−α); Σk≥Kαk = αK/(1−α).

Bellman: J*(x) = minu E{g(x,u,w) + αJ*(f(x,u,w))}; finite: J*(i) = minu{g(i,u) + αΣjpij(u)J*(j)}. Operator TJ = minuE{g+αJ(f)}; J* = lim TkJ, error ≤ αKM/(1−α).

VI: V0 = any bounded (0); Vk+1(i) = minu{g(i,u) + αΣjpij(u)Vk(j)}. Matrix: Vk+1 = min{G(0)+αP(0)Vk, G(1)+αP(1)Vk} (componentwise). Vk = optimal k-stage cost; policy = argmin. Policy often settles before values (not guaranteed).

PI: start μ0. (1) Evaluate: Jμ = gμ + αPμJμ ⇒ Jμ = (I − αPμ)−1gμ, or iterate Jμ,k+1 = gμ + αPμJμ,k. (2) Improve: μℓ+1(i) = argminu{g(i,u) + αΣpij(u)Jμℓ(j)}. Stop when μ unchanged. Finite #policies ⇒ finite steps. Actor (improve) – critic (evaluate).
2×2 inverse: [a b; c d]−1 = (1/(ad−bc))[d −b; −c a].

VI vs PI: VI cheap per iteration, many iterations, values converge asymptotically. PI: expensive evaluation (linear solve), few iterations, exact policy in finite steps. Variants: truncated evaluation (optimistic PI), asynchronous updates (only visited states). Rover: α=0.9 ⇒ μ=(0,1,0); α=0.96 ⇒ μ=(0,1,1) (drive at bottom too). Average cost: lim (1/N)E Σg.

5 · Approximate DP

Value-space approx.: ũk ∈ argminu{gk(xk,u) + J̃k+1(fk(xk,u))}, forward only, no backward pass. J̃ = J ⇒ optimal.

Rollout: J̃k+1 = cost of running a base (heuristic) policy from xk+1 to the end. Policy improvement property: rollout cost ≤ base cost. TSP (A start): AB100 AC300 AD100 AE75 BC50 BD75 BE125 CD100 CE125 DE50. Nearest neighbour gives AEDBCA = 550; rollout with N.N. base: stage 1 B(375) C(550) D(625) E(550) → B; stage 2 C375 D650 E675 → C; stage 3 D375 E425 → D ⇒ ABCDEA = 375 (optimal here, not always).

Stochastic: J̃ approx + approximate E. Certainty-equivalent control: replace wk by a single w̄k (usually E wk) ⇒ deterministic problem; optimal for LQG.
ℓ-step lookahead: min over uk, μk+1…μk+ℓ−1 of E{Σ g + J̃k+ℓ}; apply first control only (like MPC). Larger ℓ compensates poor J̃, costs more. Truncated rollout: base policy for ℓ−1 steps + terminal J̃ (often J̃=0 fine if ℓ large). Infinite: μ̃(x) ∈ argmin E{g + αJ̃(f)}.

Parametric J̃(x,r); features φ(x) (handcrafted, aggregation); linear J̃ = rTφ(x) (r = weights). Train on (xs, βs): min Φ(r) = ½Σs(J̃(xs,r) − βs)². Linear ⇒ convex: r̂ = (ΣφφT)−1Σβsφ (least squares). GD: rℓ+1 = rℓ − γℓΣsφ(xs)(rℓTφ(xs) − βs); step γ decreasing; SGD = one random sample per step. Finite horizon: fit rk backwards for each k.

6 · Reinforcement learning (model-free, tabular)

Agent applies u, observes cost g and next state x′; no model f. Alternative: system ID then MPC/DP.

TD(0) prediction of Jμ: J(xk) ← J(xk) + δk[g + αJ(xk+1) − J(xk)]. TD target = g + αJ(xk+1); TD error = target − J(xk). Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation.

Q-factors: Qμ(x,u) = E{g(x,u,w) + αJμ(f(x,u,w))}; J*(x) = minuQ*(x,u); μ*(x) = argminuQ*(x,u). Q-Bellman: Q*(x,u) = E{g + α minu′Q*(f(x,u,w),u′)}.

Sarsa (on-policy): Q(x,u) ← Q(x,u) + δ[g + αQ(x′,u′) − Q(x,u)], u′ = action actually taken next (ε-greedy). Data (x,u,g,x′,u′) = S,A,R,S,A.
Q-learning (off-policy): Q(x,u) ← Q(x,u) + δ[g + α minu′Q(x′,u′) − Q(x,u)]. Learns greedy Q* while acting ε-greedy.
ε-greedy: greedy argmin Q w.p. 1−ε, random u ∈ U w.p. ε ⇒ P(greedy) = 1−ε+ε/|U|. Exploration vs exploitation. εk decreasing, e.g. 1/k or max(1/k, 0.01).
Convergence: all (x,u) visited infinitely often, δk>0, Σδk=∞, Σδk²<∞ (δk=1/k ✓, constant δ ✗, 1/k² ✗), ε → 0 (greedy in limit). Constant ε ⇒ Sarsa converges to Q of the ε-greedy policy. Terminal-state Q = 0 initially.
Rewards instead of costs: maximise (use max, argmax), or cost = −reward. Deep RL: Q̂(x,u;r), minimise E[(g + α min Q(x′,·;r) − Q(x,u;r))²] by GD. Learning ≠ guaranteed closed-loop stability.

Hand-calc table: step | (x,u) | g | (x′,u′) | Qold | target g+αQ(x′,u′) | Qnew = Qold + δ(target − Qold). Always use the current table value (updates from earlier steps/episodes carry forward).

7 · Answer recipes

Stochastic DP by hand: (1) JN = gN. (2) Substitute x′ = f(x,u,w) into Jk+1. (3) Expand squares, take E (w-terms: E w = 0, E w² = σ²). (4) d/du = 0 → u*; check 2nd derivative > 0. (5) Plug u* back → Jk. (6) Repeat to k=0. State policy μk(x) and J0(x0).

Finite MDP DP / VI step: for each state i and each u: g(i,u) + αΣjpij(u)V(j) (α=1 for finite-horizon DP). Take min, record argmin. Show a table: state | u=0 value | u=1 value | min | policy.

PI step: write linear eqs J(i) = g(i,μ(i)) + αΣpij(μ(i))J(j); solve; then improvement table as above; stop when policy repeats.

Sarsa / Q-learning by hand: list transitions from episodes (x,u,r,x′,u′); table of all Q = 0; update one transition at a time in order; carry the table into the next episode; final table; greedy policy (min for cost, max for reward); explain ties (one-step lookahead r + α·best Q(next), or note more episodes needed).
