MPC explained like you're five

🚗 Driving with headlights (receding horizon)

At night you can only see a few metres ahead. You plan for what you can see, drive a little, then look again and re-plan. You never follow an old plan blindly, because the road may have changed.

In your P1: the crane re-solved its QP every 0.1 s and only ever used uMPCout(1), the first move of each plan.

⚖️ Q vs R: two things you hate

Q says how much you hate being away from the target. R says how much you hate pushing hard. Big Q/R: rush there, push hard. Small Q/R: stroll there gently.

In your P1: Q = I with LQR flung the load to 46°. Putting 500 on θ and 50 on ω told the controller "I really hate swinging", and the swing dropped to 8.7°.

🏁 QF: a promise about after the window

Your headlights end somewhere. QF is a price on where you are when they end, so the plan doesn't park you at the edge of a cliff just past what you can see. A good QF is what makes MPC stable.

In your P1: with no terminal term and only a 1 s window, the cart rushed, saw the target too late and the QP went infeasible.

🚧 Constraints: rules, not wishes

LQR can only be persuaded to stay safe by changing weights. MPC treats the limits as hard rules (≤ 3 m/s, ≤ 10° swing), so it can drive right up to them and no further.

In your P1: LQR played safe and took 7.3 s; MPC rode exactly on the 10° and 50°/s limits and took 5.4 s.

Deterministic DP explained like you're five

🔙 Plan the trip backwards

Stand at the destination and ask every city one question: "how much does it cost to get from here to the end, at best?" Write that on a price tag. Cities next to the end are easy; each earlier city just adds its road cost to the next city's tag and keeps the cheapest.

In your P2: tags E 807, F 450, G 603, then B 730, C 854, D 1056, then A = 1063 via B.

🧩 Principle of optimality

If the best route from A to H goes through F, then the part from F to H must itself be the best route from F. Otherwise you could swap in a better ending and beat the "best" route. That's why the tags can be reused.

In your P2: all three best paths go through F, the cheapest "hub" into H.

🍬 The greedy trap

Grabbing the cheapest road right now is like eating all your sweets first: nice now, painful later. Always compare road cost + price tag of where it leads.

In your P2: A→D (230) looked cheapest but led to a 1305 trip, 5th of 9.

💥 Curse of dimensionality

DP is exact, but the number of price tags can explode. A salesperson visiting 20 cities has about 6×1016 possible tours; DP needs about 4×108 steps. At 50 cities even DP gives up, so we approximate (tab 05).

In your P2: the TSP DP state was (where am I, which cities are left).

Stochastic DP explained like you're five

□ = you choose · ○ = chance chooses. Average at each ○, take the best at each □. (Your P3 Part B.)

🎲 Choose before the dice, react after

Some choices are made before luck shows its hand, so you judge them by their average. Later choices are made after you see what happened, so you just pick the best.

In your P3: East or West is chosen on averages (1210.4 vs 1013), but at D or E Tom simply picks the cheapest next city.

📜 A policy, not a plan

A plan says "go A, D, F, H". A policy says "if you end up at D go to F; if at E go to G". With randomness you need the rule for every outcome, because you don't know where you'll be.

In your P3: the output ['West','G','F','F','G',…] is exactly that list of rules.

📊 "Expected" means "on average over many trips"

1063 is what Tom pays on average; any single trip on A-B-F-H can cost from 430 to 2030. DP with E{·} doesn't care about that spread, only the average.

In your P3: spreading B→F's costs but keeping the mean changed nothing. Moving the mean changed the path.

🌫️ Noise is a tax you can't dodge

If the state is pushed by noise w with variance σ², a squared cost picks up an extra σ² you can't control: E{(a+w)²} = a² + σ². The best control is the same as without noise, but the cost goes up by σ² per stage.

2024 Q2: exactly this, which is why J0 = β(x0² + 3σ²).

🅿️ Stopping problems: take it or keep looking

Parking: take this spot, or gamble that a better one is ahead? Compare "stop now" with "the average cost of carrying on". The answer is usually a threshold: keep looking until about here, then take the first one you see.

In your P3: with c = 1 it was "take the first free spot"; with cheap search the threshold moved later (k* = 1, 5, 7, 8).

Infinite-horizon DP explained like you're five

💸 Discounting: tomorrow's sweets are worth less

A sweet today beats a sweet next week. α = 0.9 means each step into the future counts for 90% of the one before. It also keeps "forever" sums finite.

In your P4: V(0,0) = −1 − 0.9 − 0.81 − … = −4.0951 for a 6-move path.

📣 Value iteration = a rumour spreading from the goal

Each sweep, every cell asks its neighbours "how good is it next to you?" and updates itself. Good news travels outward one step per sweep until nothing changes.

In your P4: 7 sweeps on the 4×4 deterministic lake; 180 on the slippery one, because slip probabilities make the news travel slowly.

🔁 Policy iteration = try, score, improve

Pick any rule. Score it fully (how good is every cell if I always follow it?). Change each cell to its best move given those scores. Repeat until nothing changes. Few rounds, but each one is heavy.

In your P4: PI needed only 5 rounds on the slippery lake, compared with 180 VI sweeps, and reached the same answer.

🧊 Plan for the slip

On ice you don't always go where you aim. The smart move next to a hole is the one whose accidental slides are safe, even if that means pushing into a wall.

In your P4: the "walk straight" policy reached the goal 4.7% of the time on ice; the ice-aware one reached it 74.7% of the time.

Approximate DP explained like you're five

♟️ Look a few moves ahead, then guess

A chess player can't compute the whole game. They look 2–3 moves ahead exactly, then judge the board with a rule of thumb (J̃). Better rule of thumb, or deeper look, means better play.

In your P5: straight-line distance as J̃; ℓ = 3 was enough to play perfectly.

🪤 A bad guess can trap you

"Closer as the crow flies" isn't always closer: walls and swamps exist. A heuristic that ignores them can walk you in circles.

In your P5: (3,0) ↔ (3,1) looped forever because the −10 cells were invisible to the distance guess.

🧑‍🏫 Rollout: ask a simple helper

For each option, ask a simple helper (e.g. "always go to the nearest city") to finish the job, and see what it would cost. Pick the option whose finish is cheapest. You can never do worse than the helper alone.

In your P2/P5 world: nearest neighbour gave 58 on the AB = 50 TSP; rollout on top of it found the optimal 40.

🎚️ Features + weights: describe, don't memorise

Instead of a table with one number per state, describe each state with a few features ("how far from the goal?") and learn a weight for each. That's a few numbers instead of millions.

In your P5: three features, weights learned [10, 10, 2], and the guess was exactly right everywhere.

🎯 Certainty equivalence: pretend the dice show their average

Replace each random thing by its average, solve the easy deterministic problem, and apply the first move. It's cheap and often very good. It is exact when the noise only affects the cost.

In your P3: mean edge costs gave the exact stochastic answer, 1063.

ELI5

Reinforcement learning explained like you're five

🎮 Learning by playing

No rulebook (no model): you learn a game by trying moves and remembering what happened. RL replaces "compute with the model" by "learn from experience".

In your P6: the agent didn't know where the cliff was; it found out by falling off.

📝 Q-table = sticky notes

For every (place, move) there's a sticky note: "how good is this?" After each move you nudge the note towards what just happened + what you now think comes next. The size of the nudge is the learning rate δ.

In your P6: Q[s,a] += lr*(td_target - Q[s,a]), the picture above.

🍜 Explore vs exploit

Always order your favourite dish and you'll never find a better one; always try new ones and you'll eat badly. ε-greedy: usually pick the best known (1 − ε), sometimes try something random (ε).

In your P6: ε = 0.5 explored so much that some runs never learned a path at all.

🧗 SARSA = careful, Q-learning = confident

SARSA learns from what it actually does next, clumsy random steps included, so it learns "I sometimes trip, stay away from the edge". Q-learning learns as if it will act perfectly from now on, so the edge looks fine.

In your P6: SARSA took a 17-step safe detour; Q-learning took the 13-step cliff edge. With ε = 0 they agree.

📉 Calm down over time

Early on, big nudges and lots of exploring. Later, small nudges and mostly the best move, or you'll never settle (Robbins–Monro: Σδ = ∞, Σδ² < ∞; ε → 0).

In your P6: resetting k each episode kept ε high forever, so SARSA never found the 13-step path.
