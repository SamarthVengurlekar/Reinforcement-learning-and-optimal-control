P1 · Model Predictive Control: the gantry crane

Topic tab: 01 MPC · 2024 exam link: Q1 (role of Q, QF, R; receding horizon)

The problem in plain words

A cart runs along a 12 m crane track with a load hanging below it, modelled as a rigid rod. A motor pushes the cart with at most 100 N. The cart must carry a container from the dock (p = 2 m) to the ship (p = 10 m) in under 10 s, and for safety the load may not swing more than 10° or faster than 50°/s.

Initial condition\(x_0=[2,0,0,0]^\top\) (at rest over the containers)
Goal\(x^*=[10,0,0,0]^\top\): over the ship, stopped, no swing, within 10 s

The angle limits do two jobs: they keep the load safe, and they keep the system in the small-angle region where the linearised model is valid.

Step 1: continuous → discrete time

Controllers run on a computer, so the model is sampled every \(T_s=0.1\) s with a zero-order hold (input held constant between samples):

\[\begin{bmatrix}A & B\\ 0 & I\end{bmatrix}=\exp\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}A_c & B_c\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}T_s\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\]

That is the line ABdisc = expm([Ac Bc; zeros(1,5)]*Ts) in your code.

Step 2: choosing Q and R (Bryson's rule)

The states have very different units (metres and radians), so start by normalising each weight with the largest value you will tolerate:

\[Q_{ii}=\frac{1}{(x_i^{\max})^2},\qquad R=\frac{1}{(u^{\max})^2}\]

Step 4: the MPC code ↔ the batch equations

Code ↔ equation

Your loop builds the prediction \(\vec x=\Phi\vec u+\Lambda x_k\) (code: Phi = Φ, Delta = Λ), with \(\bar Q=\text{blkdiag}(Q,\dots,Q)\) and \(\bar R=\text{blkdiag}(R,\dots,R)\). Tracking cost \((\vec x-\vec x^*)^\top\bar Q(\vec x-\vec x^*)+\vec u^\top\bar R\vec u\) becomes a QP in \(\vec u\):

\[H=2(\Phi^\top\bar Q\Phi+\bar R),\qquad f=2\Phi^\top\bar Q(\Lambda x_k-\vec x^*)\]

State bounds \(\vec x_{\min}\le\Phi\vec u+\Lambda x_k\le\vec x_{\max}\) are split into two "≤" rows each:

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-\Phi\\ \Phi\\ -I\\ I\end{bmatrix}}_{A_{in}}\vec u\le\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda x_k-\vec x_{\min}\\ \vec x_{\max}-\Lambda x_k\\ -\vec u_{\min}\\ \vec u_{\max}\end{bmatrix}}_{b_{in}}\]

H is fixed, but f and bin depend on the measured \(x_k\), so the QP is re-solved every sample and only uMPCout(1) is applied. That is the receding-horizon principle in one line.

Size of the QP with N = 30: N = 30 decision variables (one input per step) and \(2\cdot4N+2N=10N=300\) inequality rows. Each QP must finish within Ts = 100 ms.

Horizon0.5 s1 s2 s3 s (chosen)5 s

MPC with plain Q = I, R = 1 and N = 30 settled in 5.4 s with no overshoot, riding exactly on the 10° and 50°/s limits. The weights no longer have to enforce safety, because the constraints do that. A longer horizon than 3 s cost computation and gained nothing. With Q = diag(1,0,0,0) and a 1 s horizon the QP became infeasible at about 2.9 s: the cart rushed, and by the time the target came into view it could no longer brake within the swing limits. There is no terminal constraint, so recursive feasibility is not guaranteed.

Hand-sized version · constrained scalar MPC

Plant \(x_{k+1}=2x_k+u_k\), q = r = qF = 1, N = 1, constraint \(|u|\le 0.5\). Start at \(x_0=2\).

Unconstrained: \(J=x^2+u^2+(2x+u)^2\Rightarrow dJ/du=2u+2(2x+u)=0\Rightarrow u=-x=-2\).

Constrained: with one decision variable, J is a convex parabola in u, so the constrained optimum is the unconstrained one clipped to the bound: \(u_0=-0.5\). Then \(x_1=4-0.5=3.5\), \(u_1=-0.5\), \(x_2=6.5\): the state diverges.

Lesson: no controller can beat physics. To stop |x| growing we need \(|2x+u|\le|x|\), and the best case is \(2|x|-0.5\le|x|\iff|x|\le0.5\). Outside that region the constrained problem cannot be stabilised. This is why feasibility and terminal sets matter. (With more than one decision variable, clipping is not the QP solution in general; you need the QP solver.)

Common traps
  • Constraints are only enforced at the sample times. Simulating the continuous model between samples gave about 10.1° and 53.7°/s, slightly over the limits. Fixes: tighten the constraints (9.5°, 45°/s) or reduce Ts.
  • Running exactly on the limits leaves no robustness margin against disturbances (wind, payload mass), so hard constraints can become infeasible. Fixes: soft constraints or constraint tightening.
  • Without constraints, and with the Riccati solution as QF, MPC gives exactly the LQR law.

Exam questions from P1

P1-a · words8 marks

Compare LQR and MPC under five headings: horizon, constraints, online computation, form of the control law, and guarantees.

Show answer
  • Horizon: LQR is infinite-horizon. MPC is finite-horizon N, and the receding horizon recovers infinite-horizon behaviour.
  • Constraints: LQR cannot include them; you can only tune Q, R and hope. MPC writes them into the QP as inequalities.
  • Computation: LQR solves the Riccati equation once offline, then computes \(u=-Kx\) online. MPC solves a QP every sample, which must finish within Ts.
  • Law: LQR is a fixed linear gain. Constrained MPC is a nonlinear (piecewise-affine) feedback that changes with the active constraints.
  • Guarantees: LQR is stabilising if (A,B) is stabilisable and (A,Q½) detectable. MPC needs a suitable N, terminal cost or terminal set for stability and recursive feasibility.
P1-b · calculation6 marks

Using Bryson's rule, give Q and R for the crane with limits p 8 m (move size), v 3 m/s, θ 10°, ω 50°/s, u 100 N. Why were the final θ and ω weights made even larger than this?

Show answer

Q = diag(1/64, 1/9, 1/0.1745², 1/0.8727²) = diag(0.016, 0.11, 32.8, 1.31); R = 1/100² = 10−4. Bryson's weights met every limit but took 11.3 s, which is too slow. The input enters ω much more strongly than v (B4 ≈ −1.05 vs B2 ≈ 0.23), so swing is the active constraint. The final design raised the θ and ω weights relative to p (and used R = 1 for smoothness) to get 7.3 s while staying inside 10° and 50°/s.

P1-c · words6 marks

With a 1 s horizon and Q = diag(1,0,0,0), R = 0.01, the MPC solver reported "infeasible" part-way through the move. Explain why, and give two remedies.

Show answer

Only position error is penalised, so the optimiser drives the cart as fast as allowed. A 1 s window cannot see the target until it is close; by then braking hard enough to stop would break the swing limits, so no input sequence satisfies all constraints and the QP is infeasible. Hard constraints plus a short horizon and no terminal constraint mean recursive feasibility is not guaranteed. Remedies: a longer horizon (2–3 s worked); weights on v, θ, ω for a smooth approach; a terminal constraint or terminal set that guarantees recursive feasibility; soft constraints with slack variables.

P1-d · calculation4 marks

For a 4-state, 1-input system with box constraints on every state and the input, how many decision variables and inequality rows does the condensed MPC QP have for N = 20?

Show answer

Decision variables: N·m = 20. Inequalities: upper and lower bounds on 4 states at each of the N steps give 2·4·20 = 160, plus 2·20 = 40 for the input, so 200 rows in total (10N).

Hand-sized version · nearest neighbour vs rollout (your Q17 instance)

Change only A–B from 5 to 50. Nearest neighbour = one-step lookahead with \(\tilde J=0\): from A take the cheapest unvisited city each time. A→C (1) → D (3) → B (4) → forced back to A (50): 58. The optimum is A-C-B-D-A = 1+20+4+15 = 40, because it accepts B–C (20) to avoid the 50 edge.

Rollout with nearest-neighbour as the base heuristic: for each candidate next city, complete the tour with NN and compare totals.

  • Step 1 at A: via B → A-B-D-C-A = 58; via C → A-C-D-B-A = 58; via D → A-D-C-B-A = 88. Tie between B and C; take C.
  • Step 2 at C (A, C visited): via B → A-C-B-D-A = 40; via D → A-C-D-B-A = 58. Choose B.
  • Step 3 forced: B → D → A. Tour A-C-B-D-A = 40 = optimal.

Rollout is guaranteed to be no worse than its base heuristic (cost improvement). If the step-1 tie had gone to B, rollout would return 58: no better than NN, but never worse.

P2-b · words6 marks

Why is nearest neighbour "approximate DP with \(\tilde J=0\)", and when does it fail?

Show answer

One-step lookahead picks \(\arg\min_u[g(x,u)+\tilde J(f(x,u))]\). With \(\tilde J\equiv0\) this is "take the cheapest edge now". It ignores the future, so it fails whenever a cheap edge leads into a state where only expensive edges remain, typically the forced closing edge back to the start (A–B = 50 in the modified instance: NN 58 vs optimal 40).

P3 · Stochastic DP: random costs, random roads and the parking problem

Topic tab: 03 Stochastic DP · 2024 exam link: Q2 (expectation inside the DP)

Part A: random energy on each road

Each leg now costs one of three amounts with given probabilities, e.g. A→B: 120 (0.1), 240 (0.2), 390 (0.7). The DP takes the expectation:

\[J_k(x)=\min_u\ \mathbb E_w\{g(x,u,w)+J_{k+1}(f(x,u,w))\}\]

\(\mathbb E\{g(A,B)\}=0.1(120)+0.2(240)+0.7(390)=12+48+273=333\). Every edge mean equals the Practical 2 cost (B→F 280, F→H 450, …), so the answer is again A-B-F-H with expected cost 1063.

Why the answer didn't change

Here the randomness only affects the cost, not where you end up: \(f(x,u,w)=u\). By linearity of expectation, \(J_k(x)=\min_u(\mathbb E\{g\}+J_{k+1}(u))\), which is deterministic DP on the mean costs. This is certainty equivalence: replacing randomness by its mean is exactly optimal here. It is not exact when noise moves the state and costs are non-linear (as in 2024 Q2, where \(\mathbb E\{(x+u+w)^2\}=(x+u)^2+\sigma^2\)).

Part C: the parking problem (optimal stopping)

Parks k = 0 … N−1, each free with probability ρ. If park k is free you may park for s(k) = N − k or drive on for c = 1; if it is full you must drive. If nothing is free by the end, the multi-storey costs S = N.

\[J_N=S,\qquad J_k(\text{full})=c+\rho J_{k+1}(\text{free})+(1-\rho)J_{k+1}(\text{full}),\qquad J_k(\text{free})=\min\{s(k),\ J_k(\text{full})\}\]
Hand-sized version · first three steps (N = 10, c = 1, S = 10, ρ = 0.1)

Role of the terminal cost (your Q8)

JN (1) starts the backward recursion; (2) makes every policy well defined, since the trip must end; (3) is the "price of failure" every decision is compared against; (4) in general stands in for everything after the horizon (like QF in MPC). A poor terminal cost causes end effects near the horizon.

VI vs PI: what your timing experiments showed

  • VI = many cheap sweeps (one max per state). It converges only asymptotically, at a rate set by α: 11 iterations at α = 0.5, 180 at α = 0.99, 244 at α = 0.999.
  • PI = few expensive iterations (full policy evaluation, then greedy improvement). It terminates in finitely many steps, because there are finitely many policies and each improvement is strict: 3–7 iterations in every test.
  • The policy settles long before the values: the greedy policy was final at VI iteration 45, but the values took 238 to converge.
  • Exact policy evaluation solves the linear system \((I-\alpha P_\mu)V=r_\mu\). It was 5–16× faster at small sizes but scales as about |S|2.1 compared with |S|1.2 for VI.
  • PI tie trap: if two actions have equal Q, tiny evaluation errors make argmax flip and PI never stops (359 iterations on the 32×32 map). Fix: keep the old action unless the new one is strictly better.

Exam questions from P4

P4-a · words8 marks

The optimal policy for the deterministic Frozen Lake succeeded only 4.7% of the time on the slippery lake. Explain why, and describe how the slippery-optimal policy differs.

Show answer

The deterministic policy assumes every action does what it says, so it follows the shortest path right beside holes. On the slippery lake each move goes sideways with probability ⅔; for example "down" from (1,2) slips into a hole on either side, so it survives with probability only ⅓. These risks multiply along the path. The slippery-optimal policy is computed with the true transition probabilities: it trades path length for safety, chooses actions whose perpendicular slips cannot reach a hole, and often pushes into walls. V(start) drops from 0.951 to 0.542, but success rises to about 75%.

P4-b · words6 marks

For a very large grid, what are the deficiencies of VI and PI and what would you use instead?

Show answer

Both sweep every state and action each iteration (cost ∝ |S||A| per sweep, exact PE ∝ |S|3), need the full transition model, and store a table with one value per state. The number of states explodes with the number of state variables (curse of dimensionality). Alternatives: asynchronous or prioritised VI; approximate DP with features or neural networks; state aggregation; online methods like rollout or MPC that plan only from the current state; model-free RL (TD, Q-learning) that learns from samples of the states that actually occur.

Part B: linear feature architecture trained by gradient descent

5×5 grid, goal (4,4) worth +10, each move −1. Features \(\phi(x)=\text{normalize}([x_1,\ x_2,\ |x_1-4|+|x_2-4|])\) and \(\tilde J(x,r)=r^\top\phi(x)\).

Code ↔ equation

Target (Bellman, using the current approximation): \(\beta=\max_u[-1+r^\top\phi(f(x,u))]\), or 10 at the goal.
Gradient of \(\tfrac12(r^\top\phi-\beta)^2\): \(\nabla=\phi(x)\,(r^\top\phi(x)-\beta)\). Update: \(r\leftarrow r-\gamma\nabla\), \(\gamma=0.01\).

Hand-sized version · one SGD step

r = [0,0,0], sampled state (1,1): raw features [1, 1, 6], sum 8, so φ = [0.125, 0.125, 0.75]. Prediction rTφ = 0. Every neighbour also predicts 0, so β = −1 + 0 = −1.

∇ = φ·(0 − (−1)) = [0.125, 0.125, 0.75]; r ← r − 0.01∇ = [−0.00125, −0.00125, −0.0075].

Result: r → [10, 10, 2] with error ≈ 10−5. Why exact? On a 5×5 grid, x1 + x2 + |x1−4| + |x2−4| = 8 for every cell, so the normalisation divides by a constant and \(r^\top\phi=(10x_1+10x_2+2(8-x_1-x_2))/8=x_1+x_2+2=10-\text{distance}\), which is exactly J*. The true value lies in the span of the features. With obstacles it would not, and the architecture would only approximate.

Exam questions from P5

P5-a · words6 marks

Give three limitations of increasing the lookahead horizon ℓ to fix a poor heuristic.

Show answer
  • Computation grows exponentially (bℓ action sequences), which is a problem when re-planned online at every step or with many actions.
  • There is no guarantee for any fixed ℓ: deeper traps need longer horizons, and once ℓ covers the whole path you are just solving the DP exactly.
  • It needs a model to simulate ahead and is still limited by \(\tilde J\) at the end; heuristic policies can cycle, so the result must be checked by simulation.

P6 · Reinforcement learning: Q-learning vs SARSA on Cliff Walking

Topic tab: 06 Reinforcement Learning · 2024 exam link: Q3 (Sarsa by hand)

The environment

4×12 grid, start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, cliff in between. Every step gives −1; stepping into the cliff gives −100 and sends you back to the start. The optimal path is the 13-step one along the cliff edge (up 1, right 11, down 1).

The two update rules, from your code

Q-learning (off-policy)

td_target = r + γ·max(Q[s'])

\[Q(x,u)\leftarrow Q(x,u)+\delta\big[r+\alpha\max_{u'}Q(x',u')-Q(x,u)\big]\]

Target assumes you act greedily from x′ on, whatever action you actually take next.

SARSA (on-policy)

td_target = r + γ·Q[s', a']

\[Q(x,u)\leftarrow Q(x,u)+\delta\big[r+\alpha Q(x',u')-Q(x,u)\big]\]

u′ is the action the ε-greedy policy actually picks, including random ones.

(Notation: the course writes the learning rate as δ and the discount as α; your code used learning_rate and discount_factor = 0.99.) At the goal there is no bootstrap: target = r, which is * (not terminated) in your code.

Hand-sized version · one transition, two answers

The agent is on the cliff edge, Q(edge, right) = −2, takes "right" (r = −1) to the next edge cell x′. There Q(x′, right) = −1 and Q(x′, down) = −100. The ε-greedy policy happens to pick u′ = down (exploration). Learning rate 0.5, discount 0.99.

SARSA: target = −1 + 0.99(−100) = −100 → Q = −2 + 0.5(−100 + 2) = −51.

Q-learning: target = −1 + 0.99·max(−1, −100) = −1.99 → Q = −2 + 0.5(−1.99 + 2) = −1.995.

SARSA "remembers" that an exploring agent near the cliff sometimes falls, so the edge looks bad. Q-learning ignores the exploratory slip, so the edge looks fine.

Why more SARSA episodes didn't help (your key insight)

The template reset k = 1 at the start of every episode, so ε = 1/k was ½, ⅓, … again right beside the cliff each time: exploration never faded. SARSA converges to Q* only if the policy becomes greedy in the limit (ε → 0 over the whole learning process) and the step sizes satisfy Robbins–Monro (Σδ = ∞, Σδ² < ∞). Resetting k breaks both. With ε = 1/episode instead, SARSA found the 13-step path on 5 of 10 seeds. With ε = 0, SARSA's target equals Q-learning's (u′ is the greedy action), so they learn the same path. Exploration still happened through optimistic initialisation: Q starts at 0 and all real rewards are negative.

Exam questions from P6

P6-a · words8 marks

On Cliff Walking, Q-learning learns the path along the cliff edge but SARSA learns a safer, longer path. Explain using the update rules. Which agent earns more reward during training?

Show answer

Q-learning is off-policy: its target uses \(\max_{u'}Q(x',u')\), so it estimates the value of acting greedily, i.e. Q*, and the edge path (−13) is optimal for a greedy agent. SARSA is on-policy: its target uses the action the ε-greedy policy actually takes, so it learns the value of the exploring policy. Next to the cliff, a random "down" costs −100, and SARSA builds that risk into the edge cells' Q-values, so a detour 1–2 rows up (−15 or −17) looks better. During training (while still exploring) SARSA usually earns more reward, because it falls off less often; Q-learning's greedy path is better only once exploration stops.

P6-b · words6 marks

What happens to SARSA's learned path as constant ε increases from 0 to 0.5? What schedule gives both safety while learning and optimality in the end?

Show answer

ε = 0: same as Q-learning, the 13-step edge path. Small ε: one row of margin (15 steps). Larger ε: two rows (17 steps), and at high ε some runs never learn a path to the goal because random actions dominate the data. With constant ε, SARSA converges to the Q-factors of the ε-greedy policy, so its safety margin grows with ε. A decaying schedule (εk → 0 across the whole learning process, e.g. 1/k with k never reset, and Robbins–Monro step sizes) explores early and becomes greedy in the limit, converging to Q*.
